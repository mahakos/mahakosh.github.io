AXI4-Lite: the two bugs simulation could not see

The problem

A register slave with independent write-address and write-data channels, one-entry buffers on each, and error responses for bad addresses and read-only registers. AXI lets AW and W arrive in any order and lets the master stall any response, which is where handshake bugs hide.

What we built

  • A cocotb master that drives AW and W with independent random delays, so all three orderings happen
  • Random back-pressure on BREADY and RREADY, a monitor, a protocol checker on all five channels, and 76 coverage bins with crosses
  • Formal properties in the RTL: the master's rules assumed, the slave's asserted, proved for every reachable state by k-induction

What it caught

Two planted bugs, ARREADY going high while a read response is still pending and WREADY ignoring a full buffer, passed every simulation test. Our master issues one transaction at a time, and a real interconnect does not. The formal proof found both in under two seconds. That is the practical case for running formal next to simulation on any bus interface.

APB3: a health check on our own testbench

The problem

Version 0.1 looked finished: three tests passing and 100% coverage. But coverage closed after only 100 to 200 random transfers, one planted bug was caught by luck and another by a Python crash instead of a clear error.

What we changed

  • A clean X/Z check, so an undefined value on the bus fails with a message that points at the design
  • A directed reset-value test, so reset bugs no longer depend on a lucky seed
  • A coverage model rebuilt with register × strobe crosses: 158 bins that take about 1,100 to 1,800 transfers to close
  • Random wait states, a protocol checker and a hang timeout

What it shows

A 100% coverage number can flatter a testbench. Measuring it with planted bugs is what told us where it was weak. This is the same review we run on client blocks, and the original report is published unchanged.

SPI: the hold-time bug that got through

The problem

An SPI master with CPOL and CPHA modes, MSB- or LSB-first frames, a runtime clock divider and back-to-back transfers. Data checks alone pass even when the timing around chip select is wrong, and many real slaves fail on exactly that.

What we built

  • A slave model written from the SPI timing rules, not from the RTL, that answers with queued bytes and records MOSI
  • A checker for SCLK idle level, edge count per frame, SCLK half-period, chip-select setup and hold
  • 33 coverage bins, including a mode × bit order × divider cross

What it caught

A planted bug that released chip select right after the last clock edge survived the first version, because no check measured hold time. We added the measurement, and the bug is now caught. On a client block, that gap would have been a finding in the health check.

Sync FIFO: proved, not just simulated

The problem

FIFOs look simple and still ship with bugs at the edges: full and empty at the same time, a push accepted when full, or data corrupted after the pointers wrap. Random simulation reaches those corners slowly, if at all.

What we built

  • Invariants on the count, the flags and how far the count can move each cycle
  • A data-integrity property: any value written to any slot stays intact while live and is exactly what appears at the head
  • Cover traces for full, simultaneous push and pop, and draining after a wrap

What it caught

All five planted bugs: an inverted full flag, overflow, underflow, a wrong read pointer and memory written on a refused push. Because k-induction is a full proof, there is no seed or test length that could have missed them.

UART: testing the line, not just the logic

The problem

A UART that passes with a perfect clock can still fail in the field, where the other side runs a little fast or slow and the line picks up noise. The tests have to model the line as it really behaves.

What we built

  • A line driver that can skew the baud rate and inject parity errors, framing errors, BREAKs and start-bit glitches
  • A sink that decodes the design's transmit line on its own, without trusting the design's receiver
  • 37 coverage bins across parity modes, divisors, skew and every error kind

What it caught

All eight planted bugs, including sampling at the end of each bit instead of the middle, a missing stop bit after parity and a receiver that did not wait out a BREAK.
